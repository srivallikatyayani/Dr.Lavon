My story

How I reached where I am in life

It was the beginning of 1997. I had decided to retry my 2nd B.Sc. paramedical course in speech and hearing, with a lot of planning, when I got kicked off Mount Everest, so to say!

My ignorant mother had felt intimidated all along because I questioned why she had grossly underprivileged me, her girl child, whereas she had given my brother a very special, warm upbringing. A rift had been created between us by my sadist brother, who was exceedingly manipulative, horribly patriarchal, and whose role model was Adolf Hitler.

When I protested against injustice I was locked up at home, without being sent to college, for a whole year when I desperately desired to study. This was based on very wrong advice given to my mother by two so-called psychologists and a psychiatrist who knew nothing about mental health or my condition. I became chronically symptomatic due to that year of incarceration, and decided to launch my fight for recovery.

I challenged my psychiatrist and told her I wasn't a 'medical failure'.

I said I would come back as a women's mental health specialist in exactly 10 years, with a Bachelor's, a Master's and a PhD in Women's Mental Health, and propound a new way to heal women with mental health issues instead of keeping them in sickness mode.

In exactly 10 years I won the challenge.

I converted both my psychiatrist and my mother into my greatest supporters, and became India's First Recovery Specialist with a PhD in Women's Mental Health.

In 2009 I was the only person from India fully funded and selected to attend the Global Mental Health Summit in Greece. Fellowships, talks and research papers took me across the world, helping others heal and carrying the message of recovery. Today I am creating a new cadre of recovery specialists and experiential experts from among people undergoing mental health issues.

A New Leadership for the New World.

One-on-one coaching

Live a life of recovery and don't get relegated into a sickness slot.

The first step is a cup of lemon juice or herbal tea, whichever you choose, and a heart-to-heart talk.

There are no compulsions. It is to see if we are meant to continue on a journey that can potentially be life-changing and help in recovery. If you then feel ready to use my expertise in full recovery and true empowerment, we'll begin one-on-one coaching.

I operate on a very wide canvas: from recovery, to building a career in mental health, to being alive and vibrant with every breath you take, achieving what you are truly passionate about, and finding your life's true purpose based on your unique talents and potential.

What is one-on-one coaching?

Just like a surgeon who knows what questions to ask to understand where the problem is, I know exactly what questions to ask to determine where you're getting stuck mentally, what needs to be done to help you out, and how to make you willing to act.

My sessions are a safe, non-judgmental place to discuss your issues and ambitions. Then I give you tools to take action, and a proper plan to get yourself to exactly where you want to be in life.

What results can I expect?

We work as a team to get you clarity on your life's true purpose, the courage to go after it, and the consistency to achieve it.

I'll study your goals and help you build a step-by-step, actionable plan, with ways of promoting accountability every step of the way. You'll gain tools for self-correction and self-improvisation that help in all your deepest relationships.

Finally, you will walk away with a life and career that you love.

How is this different from traditional therapy?

Traditional 'counselling' and 'therapy' don't go to the crux of how the client is feeling and what is happening deep within, because they are not born out of experiential expertise. They put too much intra-psychic emphasis on recovery, when the external environment may actually be inducing the behavior. We go deep into what is happening to the client, plus the social, political, legal, cultural and economic barriers to recovery, and the role caregivers and family play, along with the support they themselves need.

If a woman is beaten by her husband every day, she needs to be told what's going wrong, but we also need extra-psychic change: the husband must be taught and compelled to show civilized behavior, with checks to ensure this. We don't blame the victim. Change must happen within the individual, and in the social environment just as much.

We don't operate using patriarchal road maps. We don't say a woman was raped because she provoked the man. We don't say marriage or joint families are an insurance against mental breakdown; overly political joint families could actually be inducing breakdowns. We don't say women have an innate propensity for getting depressed.

We don't say mental illnesses are 'treatable' but not curable. We give clients the tools to heal themselves, for instance by being sensitive to body-talk. A lot is done very differently in Dr. Lavanya's support system, for both caregivers and clients.

What readers say

Dr. Lavanya Seshasayee is a psychotherapist with a Ph.D. in Women's Mental Health. An unapologetic feminist, she has reinvented herself as a legal self-advocate helping individuals reclaim their lives lost to mental illness. Her revolutionary Ph.D. thesis won worldwide acclaim. She believes that medical training needs to change, especially where psychiatry is concerned, and has pushed for client-empowering perspectives as the Founder of the Global Women's Recovery Movement and Global Fight for Recovery.

In this deeply personal narrative, she recounts her interactions with psychiatrists, legal experts, policymakers, psychologists, and alternative healing practitioners. She hilariously chronicles her encounters with women's activists, literary writers, and academia in mental health. The book is balanced with heartwarming stories of user survivors and insights into schizophrenia from an insider's perspective.

Dr. Lavanya Seshasayee tells it all with unflinching honesty. Her book is for everybody seeking an inspiring, motivating, and enlightening read on responding to extreme adversity in life and emerging a winner.

Lavanya Seshasayee has written a captivating book. It is a personal story, but at the same time full of universal meaning and perspectives on many different levels. The vivid text arouses a world of thoughts in yourself, when reading it. It reminds me of the great English author Virginia Woolf, especially her novel Mrs Dalloway. It's written in the same stream of consciousness. The story travels forward and back in time, and in and out of the characters' mind to construct an image of the main character Aditi's life and the social structure surrounding it.

Just as Virginia Woolf, the author has deep personal experience of that state of mind which is called psychosis or even schizophrenia. Both authors let you in their stories, experience what this state of mind is really about. That it is not an illness, and not something totally irrational, but a reaction to unbearable experiences earlier in life. It also gives hope to the reader, because Lavanya Seshasayee shows in her book that there is a way out of the distress, although this seems impossible, when you are in the midst of it. If you find the meaning of your distress, you can also find a way out of it. I predict that Lavanya Seshasayee will become the Virginia Woolf of India!

Mental disorders

Mental disorders (or mental illnesses) are conditions that affect your thinking, feeling, mood and behavior. They may be occasional or long-lasting (chronic). They can affect your ability to relate to others and function each day.

Some types of mental disorders

  • Anxiety disorders, including panic disorder, obsessive-compulsive disorder and phobias
  • Depression, bipolar disorder and other mood disorders
  • Eating disorders
  • Personality disorders
  • Post-traumatic stress disorder
  • Psychotic disorders, including schizophrenia

What causes mental disorders?

There is no single cause for mental illness. A number of factors can contribute to risk:

  • Your life experiences, such as stress or a history of abuse, especially in childhood
  • A very bad social upbringing traumatizing the brain
  • A traumatic brain injury
  • A mother's exposure to viruses or toxic chemicals while pregnant
  • Use of alcohol or recreational drugs
  • Having a serious medical condition like cancer
  • Having few friends, and feeling lonely or isolated

How are mental disorders diagnosed?

  1. A medical history
  2. A physical exam and possibly lab tests, if your provider thinks other medical conditions could be causing your symptoms
  3. A psychological evaluation, where you answer questions about your thinking, feelings and behaviors

Schizophrenia

What it is, what causes it, how it shows up, and how the Global Fight for Recovery approaches healing.

What is schizophrenia?

Schizophrenia is a chronic mental illness in which the person seems to indulge in a reality of their own, a bit distant from real life. It happens due to extreme stress when one is forced to live in an unlivable social atmosphere or pathological social environment. When you can't get out of such a control-command, fault-finding environment, and there are no environmental buffers to save you, the consequence is schizophrenia.

1% of the world population is diagnosed with schizophrenia. Dr. Lavanya has been treating schizophrenia for several years, and feels mental illnesses need the right treatment. The person experiences hallucinations and delusions which affect how they feel. It interferes with their personal life and makes it difficult for friends and family to deal with the client and treat her.

Research shows that more than 2.5 thousand people were treated for schizophrenia in 2018 in Columbia.

Schizophrenia is often confused with multiple personality disorder. It is a disconnection of thoughts from reality, and the person may seem to live in a world of imagination. The kind of imagination is usually linked to the environment they have gone through or are going through. Some people may feel completely normal for some time and then completely lose touch with others who may have a strong impact on their life journey.

It is one of the top 15 causes of disability according to the Global Burden of Disease. Schizophrenia can affect people of any age, is found throughout the world, and has a wide range of symptoms and treatments. Symptoms normally start in the teens but are not very prominent; sudden changes in behavior and mood could be early signs. According to Dr. Lavanya, the symptoms become prominent as age progresses and must be treated immediately. The more severe it becomes, the more difficult it is to treat.

What causes schizophrenia?

There are many factors. Just one, or more than one, may play a role. In 2017, 0.26% of males and 0.25% of females had schizophrenia, which has now increased to 1% of the general population.

1. Genes and hormones

Genes and hormones have no part to play here, although according to a study by Harvard Medical School, if one parent has schizophrenia there is little possibility that the child will have it; if both parents have it, it is more likely (50% probability).

Here is what Dr. Lavanya Seshasayee has to say about this whole thing. As for my argument that schizophrenia is not genetically inherited, but only happens through faulty familial role-modelling, faulty child-rearing practices and painful social conditioning, this is what I would like to say.

If schizophrenia were genetically inherited, then the identical twin of somebody with schizophrenia would always have schizophrenia. Research has shown that this is not the case.

Identical twins, to start with, have exactly the same genes. Put one of them in a pathological social environment and she develops a mental illness such as schizophrenia; put the other in a very nurturing and conducive environment and she becomes an achiever. When genes are identical but social environments differ, individuals grow into totally different personalities. To prove that social environment causes schizophrenia, you need two people with the same genes but different environments. There have been umpteen studies to prove this.

To prove that faulty genes are the cause, you would need to compare two individuals with different genes but the same social environment. The crux of my argument is that two sets of genes can be exactly the same, but no two social environments can be exactly the same, unless the two individuals grow up in environments that are laboratory-controlled for all variables. Even identical twins grow up in societies, not laboratories. So how can you say the environments of two identical twins were exactly the same? Obviously they were different, and that is why one acquired schizophrenia while the other didn't. The healthy twin had some protective social-environmental buffer that prevented her from succumbing; her sibling did not.

My point is simple. Two sets of genes can be exactly the same, but no two social environments can be exactly the same.

Again, to say that schizophrenia occurs in one of a pair of non-identical twins because of a genetic difference in vulnerability, we would need two social environments that are exactly the same, and this is not possible either. So how can you tell whether a genetic or a social-environmental difference is responsible?

In some cases it is hypothesized that identical twins raised in two totally different environments both develop schizophrenia, and that genes are therefore a very powerful factor. But even here, their environments have never been totally the same or laboratory-controlled, so it is again the same genes with different socio-cultural environments. It is social-environmental pathology that is responsible for their similarity. And these twins are not similar in all respects; their differing environments also contributed to their differences.

Also, clients' parents should ask clients why they behave the way they do. Instead, they ask a psychiatrist who knows very little about the client's condition despite having read tons of theory on schizophrenia, because the psychiatrist hasn't been through it. Schizophrenia should be addressed by the users and survivors who have experienced it, yet everybody other than the suffering individual thinks they are entitled to speak on behalf of the one who is actually going through it and knows what it really is.

2. Brain function

The medical model says changes in the brain due to changes in the body, like puberty, may contribute to this illness, along with changes in neurotransmitters such as dopamine or glutamate, which are supposed to be reasons for hallucinations, and changes in the central nervous system which may alter how the brain works. However, none of this is conclusively proved.

Many scientists believe that because it is a brain disorder, alterations in brain circuits are behind it. According to research, problems in brain connectivity have been a reason for its development, and brain alteration can happen because of several external factors.

Environmental factors play an important role not only in developing but also in worsening the disease. Dr. Lavanya feels the biggest factor is a high-stress condition, along with nutritional problems and financial conditions. Symptoms of schizophrenia

The symptoms are not visible quickly. They are usually seen clearly in the late teens; small changes during the teens, in behavior and family interaction, are not prominent enough to be detected. Gradual changes in brain functioning and mood are seen. In Denmark in 2018, 13,000 women and around 18,000 men were diagnosed with schizophrenia.

Early symptoms include lack of sleep and lack of improvement in life. It disconnects the client from the social world, and the person is always stressed.

Positive symptoms

Also called psychotic symptoms, these include hallucinations and delusions. Hallucinations make the person see or hear things that the mind makes: the feeling of someone touching their body, or smells not sensed by others. Delusions make the person believe wrong or false statements far from reality. Catatonia, in which the body doesn't move and stays fixed in a certain position or posture, is also a psychotic symptom.

Negative symptoms

This simply means behavior missing from normal behavior: lack of energy, of speaking and of sleep; a lack of emotions and of pleasure in life; little interest in family and personal life; and a loss of grooming sense.

Cognitive symptoms

Symptoms relating to memory. It becomes difficult to focus, concentration and memory decrease, the ability to make decisions reduces, and problems with working memory make it difficult to implement things that are learned.

Other symptoms

  • Repeating similar actions continuously for very long
  • Talking randomly without making sense of the words
  • Writing things without meaning, for no reason
  • Difficulty in having a social conversation
  • Moving quickly between thoughts in an imaginary world
  • Difficulty handling things, or forgetting things quickly
  • Sudden changes in mood and temper for no reason

If not treated, schizophrenia can cause serious problems, affecting quality of life in many ways. Symptoms may increase and change into more aggressive behavior that is harder to treat. Self-isolation and suicide attempts can create a scenario that is very difficult to control, and uncontrollable behavior can make the situation very dangerous in many cases.

Diagnosing schizophrenia

There is no particular test for this disorder, but several factors are considered. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5), published by the American Psychiatric Association, sets criteria for the symptoms and types of symptoms. The psychiatrist will first ask the person or the family about symptoms, which should include hallucinations, delusions and disturbed speaking patterns for a month or two. Other tests, including blood tests, urine tests and an MRI, may be needed to make sure no physical problem or drug addiction is causing the symptoms. The psychiatrist will then talk to you in detail and take your history to begin treatment.

Conclusion

Schizophrenia has been the most widespread disorder to date in the category of mental disorders. There are many causes, and with innovation reaching new heights, people are becoming more isolated, with stress caused by social media. Being far away from family, or facing the challenges of a career in this world, makes a person more prone to stress, which leads to the growth of symptoms.

Dr. Lavanya Seshasayee treats clients with schizophrenia and believes this illness is treatable; it takes time, depending on its level. Diagnosing it at the right time, or pointing someone who is going through it towards a recovery specialist, helps treat it and makes this world a better place to live in.

Dr. Lavanya Seshasayee's treatments combine medication with non-medical support systems, and are proven to benefit the client. Dr. Lavanya will not prescribe medication, but can refer you to a good psychiatrist. Correct diagnosis by a professional therapist, blood tests to check for deficiencies causing symptoms, working through the family issues the person is facing with all family members, and gaining deep contact with the person have been by far the best approach to treating clients with schizophrenia.

The period of treatment depends on the level of the disorder. Good family support and a positive attitude can limit the time needed, make a life-changing impact, and help a person live a gratifying life.
